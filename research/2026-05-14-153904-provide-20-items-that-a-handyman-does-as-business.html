Travel Charges — Industry Standard

Most handymen do not include travel for free. Here's how it typically breaks down.

Common Approaches

MethodTypical ChargeWhen to Use
Free within local radiusFirst 10–15 miles freeBest for marketing; absorb cost into hourly rate
Flat trip / service fee$25–$75 per visitSimple, predictable for customer
Per-mile beyond radius$0.70–$1.50/mile (round trip)Fair for occasional far jobs
Minimum service call$75–$150 (covers 1st hour + travel)Industry standard — protects against tiny jobs
Billable travel timeHalf hourly rate, or full rate after 30 minLong-distance / commercial work

Recommended Structure for a Solo Handyman

  1. 0–15 miles: Included (baked into hourly rate)
  2. 15–30 miles: $35–$50 flat trip fee
  3. 30+ miles: $1.00/mile round trip or bill drive time at $35–$45/hr (half rate)
  4. Emergency / same-day: Add $50–$100 surcharge
  5. Always enforce a minimum: $95–$125 minimum, even for a 10-minute job

Rules of Thumb

  • IRS mileage rate for 2026: ~$0.70/mile — that's your cost floor, not what you charge. Charge 1.5–2× that to cover wear, time, and gas.
  • Disclose travel fees upfront on your quote — surprise travel charges are the #1 complaint in handyman reviews.
  • Bundle visits in the same area on the same day to avoid eating travel costs.
  • For repeat customers within your service zone, keep it free — loyalty is worth more than $25.
Bottom line: Include travel within ~15 miles, charge $35–$50 flat beyond that, and never leave the driveway for less than your $95+ minimum.

Markup Structure

A healthy commercial painting bid breaks down roughly:

  • Labor: 40–50%
  • Materials: 15–20%
  • Overhead (insurance, vehicles, equipment): 15–20%
  • Profit margin: 15–25% (aim for 20%+)

Premium Charges (add to base rate)

Minimums & Fees

  • Job minimum: $500–$1,500 (don't show up for less)
  • Mobilization fee: $250–$750 for out-of-town jobs
  • Estimate / consultation: Free for jobs > $2K; $150–$300 for small bids
  • Color consultation: $75–$150/hr if not included

Rules of Thumb

  • Bid by the square foot, sell by the project — clients want one number, not a calculator.
  • Always include 2 coats in the base price; single-coat is a red flag in commercial work.
  • Material markup: 25–50% on paint and supplies.
  • Bond + insurance required on most commercial jobs > $10K (factor 2–4% of contract value).
  • Net 30 payment terms are standard — price in 1.5–2% for cash flow cost.
  • Walk every job before quoting — square footage from blueprints lies.

Square vs. Stripe vs. QuickBooks

These three get lumped together but solve different problems. Many small businesses end up using all three.

At a Glance

Square — "I need to swipe cards and run a shop"

An all-in-one payments + lightweight business platform built for in-person transactions.

Strengths

  • Free POS app + free magstripe reader to start
  • Hardware ecosystem: Square Reader, Terminal, Register, Kiosk
  • Built-in tools: appointments, invoices, payroll, gift cards, loyalty, basic inventory
  • Same-day or next-day deposits to your bank
  • No monthly fee on the basic plan

Fees (typical)

Transaction TypeRate
In-person swipe / tap / dip2.6% + $0.10
Online2.9% + $0.30
Keyed-in or invoices3.5% + $0.15
Square Invoices via card3.3% + $0.30

Weak spots

  • Account holds / freezes are a well-known complaint
  • Limited international support
  • Reporting / accounting is shallow — most users still need QuickBooks
  • Not designed for complex subscription billing

Who picks Square: coffee shops, food trucks, salons, handymen, market vendors, anyone with a phone or iPad who needs to charge a customer standing in front of them.

Stripe — "I need to charge cards from my website or app"

A developer-first payments platform for online businesses. Think of it as plumbing — powerful, programmable, mostly invisible to your customer.

Strengths

  • Best-in-class APIs and developer docs
  • Sophisticated subscription billing (Stripe Billing) — proration, dunning, trials, metered usage
  • Global: 135+ currencies, local payment methods (SEPA, iDEAL, Alipay, etc.)
  • Marketplaces / platforms (Stripe Connect) — splitting payments to multiple sellers
  • Add-ons: Radar (fraud), Tax (sales tax), Atlas (company formation), Issuing (cards), Capital (loans)
  • Stripe Terminal exists for in-person, but it's an afterthought vs. Square

Fees (typical, US)

Transaction TypeRate

Weak spots

  • Built for developers — non-technical users hit a wall fast (mitigated by Stripe Payment Links, Checkout)
  • No real POS, no hardware ecosystem
  • No accounting features at all
  • Customer support is notoriously thin for small accounts

Who picks Stripe: SaaS companies, e-commerce sites, marketplaces, anyone with engineers, anyone selling subscriptions or operating internationally.

QuickBooks — "I need to know what I earned, spent, and owe in taxes"

Accounting software by Intuit. It doesn't process payments at its core (there's an add-on called QuickBooks Payments, but that's not why anyone buys it). It tracks the money.

Strengths

  • Full general ledger / double-entry bookkeeping
  • Income & expense categorization, P&L, balance sheet, cash flow
  • Bank / credit card feeds — auto-imports transactions
  • Invoicing, bill pay, 1099 contractors, payroll (add-on)
  • Sales tax tracking, mileage tracking, receipt capture
  • Every accountant and tax preparer knows it — easy to hand off at tax time
  • Integrates with Square, Stripe, Shopify, banks, etc.

How They Actually Fit Together

Most businesses use a stack, not one tool:

┌──────────────────────┐    ┌──────────────────────┐
│  Square (in-person)  │    │   Stripe (online)    │
│   takes the money    │    │   takes the money    │
└──────────┬───────────┘    └──────────┬───────────┘
           │                           │
           └──────────┬────────────────┘
                      ▼
          ┌──────────────────────┐
          │     QuickBooks       │
          │   tracks the money   │
          │   (books & taxes)    │
          └──────────────────────┘
  • Square and Stripe answer: "How do I get paid?"
  • QuickBooks answers: "What just happened to my finances, and what do I owe?"

Which Should You Use?

Your SituationRecommended Stack
Handyman, painter, plumber — mostly in-person, some invoicesSquare + QuickBooks
E-commerce store on Shopify / WooCommerceStripe (or Shopify Payments) + QuickBooks
SaaS or subscription productStripe + QuickBooks
Brick-and-mortar retail with a small websiteSquare (for both) + QuickBooks

Rule of thumb: Pick a payment processor based on where your customers transact (in-person → Square, online → Stripe), then pipe everything into QuickBooks so your accountant doesn't quit at tax time.
